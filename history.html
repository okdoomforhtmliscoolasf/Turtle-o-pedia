Built over

The turtle body plan did not appear all at once. Fossils preserve a sequence of forms that help explain how the shell evolved.

A LIVING LINEAGE

Ancient does not mean unchanged.

Turtles have a long fossil history, but the living group is not frozen in time. Modern turtles diversified into ecological roles on land and in freshwater and marine environments.

The exact placement of some fossil relatives continues to be studied. The sequence below is a simplified guide to important finds, not a complete family tree.

A SHELL TAKES SHAPE

Four moments
in the fossil record.

01

MIDDLE TRIASSIC

Pappochelys

Known for a short torso and broadened ribs. It lacked the complete shell seen in living turtles, making it useful for understanding early stages of the body plan.

02

LATE TRIASSIC

Eorhynchochelys

Another early stem-turtle form with expanded ribs and a distinctive combination of primitive and more turtle-like features.

03

LATE TRIASSIC · ABOUT 220 MILLION YEARS AGO

Odontochelys

Shows a complete bony plastron but an incomplete carapace — an important glimpse of a turtle-like animal before the full shell arrangement.

04

LATE TRIASSIC · ABOUT 210 MILLION YEARS AGO

Proganochelys

One of the earliest known stem turtles with a complete carapace and plastron. Its shell was already recognisably turtle-like.

“A shell is not simply armour added on top. It is a reorganisation of the body.”
